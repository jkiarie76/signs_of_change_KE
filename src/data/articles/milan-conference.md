The Second International Congress on Education of the Deaf met in Milan, Italy, from 6 to 11 September 1880. The conference brought together educators to debate approaches to educating Deaf children.

A major outcome was a series of resolutions favoring oral education over manual methods. This strengthened the position of oralism in many Deaf schools and contributed to the long-term suppression of sign languages in educational settings.

The consequences were significant. In many places, Deaf educators and students experienced a dramatic reduction in the use of sign language at school. The decision became an important part of Deaf history because it demonstrated how educational policy could affect not only classroom practice, but also language, identity and community.

The legacy of Milan is still discussed today because Deaf communities and educators continue to examine the importance of accessible education and the right to language.

Understanding this history helps explain why the recognition and protection of sign languages remain important issues in Deaf education.
